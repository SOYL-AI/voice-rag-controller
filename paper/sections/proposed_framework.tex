\section{Proposed Framework}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{subsec:problem_formulation}
A real time voice-based dialogue system is responding to a sequence of user turns. At each turn, before generating a spoken response, the system can optionally retrieve external documents to ground its answer.
The controller governs two key decisions: \textit{whether} to retrieve knowledge and \textit{when} to initiate retrieval relative to the user's ongoing speech. While fetching knowledge improves response accuracy, it adds latency, thereby consuming computational resources.

Formally, at dialogue turn $t$, the system receives a user query $q_t$. The system maintains a belief state $b_t$, which estimates the necessity of external knowledge based on model uncertainty and the current dialogue context. Based on $b_t$, the controller selects an action $a_t \in \mathcal{A}$ from a multi-timescale action space defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A} = \{\text{skip}\} \cup \{(\text{retrieve}, \tau) \mid \tau \in \{\text{immediate}, \text{fixed\_early\_proxy}, \text{sentence\_end}\}\}
\end{equation}
where $\text{skip}$ generates a response using internal model knowledge without retrieval, and $(\text{retrieve}, \tau)$ initiates document retrieval at a specific temporal trigger point $\tau$ relative to the user's speech.
Executing action $a_t$ yields three measurable outcomes: answer quality $Q(a_t)$, response latency $L(a_t)$, and computational cost $C(a_t)$.

The controller aims to select $a_t$ to maximize a scalarized utility objective $U(a_t)$ that rewards high answer quality while penalizing latency and computational cost:
\begin{equation}
U(a_t) = Q(a_t) - \lambda_l L(a_t) - \lambda_c C(a_t)
\end{equation}  
where $\lambda_l \ge 0$ and $\lambda_c \ge 0$ are penalty weights setting the relative prices of latency and computation against quality. Rather than learning a policy via reinforcement learning, the controller evaluates the expected utility across the candidate action space and selects the optimal action via direct maximization:
\begin{equation}
a_t^* = \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \mathbb{E}[U(a) \mid b_t]
\end{equation}

A primary open question in this setup is how the retrieval and timing decisions should be structured. These decisions can be made jointly, optimizing retrieval selection and temporal execution in a single step (our \textit{coupled} controller) or sequentially, deciding whether to retrieve first and determining timing as an independent sub-problem (the \textit{decomposed} baseline, B9).

\subsection{Belief State}
\label{subsec:belief_state}

The belief state $b_t \in [0, 1]$ represents the controller's estimate of whether external knowledge retrieval is necessary to answer query $q_t$. Rather than assuming retrieval is always beneficial, the controller assesses the model's internal confidence during an initial closed-book generation attempt without retrieval.
The raw uncertainty signal $s_t$ is defined as the mean per-token entropy (measured in nats) of the model's output probability distribution during closed-book generation:
\begin{equation}
s_t = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} H(P(y_i \mid y_{<i}, q_t))
\end{equation}
A low entropy $s_t$ shows high model confidence which suggests that retrieval may be unnecessary, while a high entropy indicates uncertainty or missing facts, increasing the expected value of retrieving external grounding.
To transform this unbounded entropy metric into a calibrated probability of retrieval necessity $P(\text{retrieval needed})$, we apply a logistic calibration function fit empirically in Experiment 1 (E1):
\begin{equation}
b_t = \sigma(w \cdot s_t + c)
\end{equation}
where $\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$ is the standard sigmoid function, $w \approx 11.29$ is the slope, and $c \approx -3.63$ is the intercept. This calibrated belief $b_t$ directly determines the expected Value of Information ($\text{VoI}$). In the decision framework, $b_t$ scales the expected quality gain term $Q(a_t)$ in the scalarized utility objective (Section~\ref{subsec:problem_formulation}), allowing the system to weigh the quality boost against latency and compute costs.

\textit{Methodological Note on Signal Selection:} During Experiment 1's (E1) validation, maximum per-token entropy ($\text{max\_entropy\_nats}$) achieved a higher discriminative ranking performance (AUC $0.978$) than mean per-token entropy (AUC $0.925$).
However, max entropy suffers from extreme bimodal saturation across token sequences, making it poorly suited for continuous probability calibration.
We intentionally selected mean per-token entropy $\text{mean\_entropy\_nats}$ as the operational signal $s_t$ due to its smooth distribution. Both signals comfortably exceed the pre-established $0.70$ AUC validation gate threshold.

\subsection{Multi-Timescale Action Space}
\label{subsec:action_space}

As introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:problem_formulation}, the action space couples the binary retrieval selection with a temporal execution trigger $\tau$. When retrieval is invoked, the controller selects when to begin document fetching relative to the user's unfolding speech stream. We consider three different timing policies for $\tau$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\text{sentence\_end}$: Retrieval initiates only after the user completes their current sentence, using the complete query transcript as a baseline.
    \item $\text{immediate}$: Retrieval initiates as soon as an initial partial transcript becomes available, allowing the system to fetch documents in parallel with the user's ongoing speech.
    \item $\text{fixed\_early\_proxy}$: Retrieval initiates at a fixed early temporal milestone within the user's speech timeline.
\end{enumerate}

Initiating retrieval while the user is still speaking allows execution to overlap with speech input, reducing the exposed latency $L_{\text{exposed}}(\tau)$ experienced by the user:
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{exposed}}(\tau) = L_{\text{retrieval}} - \text{overlap}(\tau)
\end{equation}
Across system logs, the unmitigated retrieval latency is $L_{\text{retrieval}} \approx 672\text{ ms}$. The empirically measured speech overlap values are $0\text{ ms}$ for $\text{sentence\_end}$ by construction, $\approx 50\text{ ms}$ for $\text{immediate}$, and $\approx 188\text{ ms}$ for $\text{fixed\_early\_proxy}$. Thus earlier timing policies shave a modest portion off the exposed latency.

Crucially, empirical measurements indicate that the effect of timing on quality is neutral in this operational setting. Although retrieving against partial transcripts introduces a theoretical risk of degraded query intent, the measured partial query risk was negligible ($\approx 0$) across all three policies and earlier execution altered neither document selection nor answer accuracy. Consequently, answer quality $Q(a_t)$ depends on whether the system retrieves and not on the temporal trigger $\tau$.
We term this framework a \textit{multi-timescale} action space because it operates across two temporal granularities: the discrete turn-level decision of whether external knowledge is needed, and the sub-turn, fine-grained timeline of the user's utterance that governs execution timing.

Because a turn's action jointly specifies retrieval selection and timing, these decisions can be structured in two ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ours (Coupled Controller):} Selects $a_t$ via a single joint $\arg\max$ over all candidates in $\mathcal{A}$, evaluating retrieval and timing combinations under a shared utility objective.
    \item \textbf{Baseline B9 (Decomposed Controller):} Factors the decision into two sequential sub-problems: first, a binary retrieval selection, and second, a timing selection conditioned on the retrieval decision. This decomposition allows for independent optimization of each sub-problem but may miss joint utility interactions.
\end{itemize}
Both architectures share identical objective functions, uncertainty belief signals, and cost parameters ($\lambda_l, \lambda_c$), differing strictly in whether decisions are composed jointly or decomposed. Because timing adjustments yield most latency reductions ($\le 188\text{ ms}$) without affecting accuracy, the decision task reduces primarily to whether retrieval is justified under prevailing prices. Our evaluation tests whether joint coupling yields a superior trade-off profile compared to decomposed decision making.

\subsection{Latency- and Compute-Priced Objective}
\label{subsec:priced_objective}

Recalling the decision framework introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:problem_formulation}, the controller's objective is to maximize a scalarized utility function:
\begin{equation}
    U(a_t) = Q(a_t) - \lambda_l L(a_t) - \lambda_c C(a_t)
\end{equation}
To ensure interpretable optimization across trade-off frontiers, each term is grounded in explicit operational units:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Quality Gain $Q(a_t) \in [0, 1]$:} A dimensionless measure representing the expected accuracy improvement from retrieval. With the utility scale normalized to $1.0$, $Q(a_t)$ equals the calibrated belief $b_t$ (Section~\ref{subsec:belief_state}), where $1.0$ indicates that retrieval turns an incorrect closed-book response into a correct grounded response, and $0.0$ indicates zero value added.
    \item \textbf{Latency $L(a_t)$:} Measured in seconds of exposed response delay $L_{\text{exposed}}(\tau)$. Consequently, the multiplier $\lambda_l \ge 0$ represents the marginal quality penalty per second of latency ($\text{quality}/\text{second}$).
    \item \textbf{Compute Cost $C(a_t)$:} Measured in discrete retrieval units, where executing a retrieval costs $1.0$ and a $\text{skip}$ action costs $0$. Thus, $\lambda_c \ge 0$ represents the marginal quality penalty per retrieval execution ($\text{quality}/\text{retrieval}$).
\end{itemize}

The latency parameters are grounded in empirical system logs spanning $751$ completed retrieval executions. The unmitigated retrieval duration is $L_{\text{retrieval}} \approx 0.672\text{ s}$ ($672\text{ ms}$), and the speech-overlap durations for $\text{sentence\_end}$, $\text{immediate}$, and $\text{fixed\_early\_proxy}$ are $0\text{ s}$, $\approx 0.050\text{ s}$, and $\approx 0.188\text{ s}$, respectively (Section~\ref{subsec:action_space}).

\textit{Methodological Note on Cost Parameters:} While retrieval execution cost $C(a_t) = 1.0$ and latency parameters are measured directly, cancelled or re-dispatched early retrieval attempts incur a fractional cost governed by a $\text{waste\_rate}$ parameter. We explicitly note that $\text{waste\_rate}$ serves as an unmeasured placeholder parameter in our implementation, representing the sole un-grounded constant in our cost model.

Crucially because partial query risk was empirically measured at $\approx 0$ across all timing policies, the quality channel remains identically $1.0$ regardless of trigger timing. This empirical result is an architectural strength: the comparison between the coupled controller (Ours) and the decomposed baseline (B9) cleanly isolates the latency channel, ensuring that measured differences in utility cannot be confounded by quality degradation from early dispatch.

To map the efficiency frontiers, controllers are evaluated across a derived grid of $26$ penalty points for $\lambda_l$ spanning $\approx 0.022$ to $1.713\text{ quality/s}$, holding compute price fixed at $\lambda_c = 0.05\text{ quality/retrieval}$. This grid is generated systematically by tiling across the empirical value-of-information distribution of $b_t$ rather than through manual selection. Because of $b_t$'s near-binary concentration, the region of potential divergence between joint and decomposed optimization is naturally restricted to specific price bands.


\subsection{Decision Rule}
\label{subsec:decision_rule}

Both the coupled (Ours) and decomposed (B9) controllers share the same fundamental decision primitive. They evaluate actions using the identical scalarized utility objective defined in Section~\ref{subsec:priced_objective}, and they rely on the same value-of-information (VoI) estimates and measured cost constants. They differ exclusively in how they compose the retrieval and timing sub-decisions.
\textbf{Ours (Coupled):} The coupled controller resolves the action in a single, unified step. It enumerates the full joint candidate set containing exactly four actions:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A} = \{\text{skip}\} \cup \left( \{\text{retrieve}\} \times \{\text{sentence\_end}, \text{immediate}, \text{fixed\_early\_proxy}\} \right)
\end{equation}
Each candidate is scored under the shared objective, with the utility of the $\text{skip}$ candidate fixed at $0$. The controller then selects the optimal action via a global maximization:
\begin{equation}
a_t^* = \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \mathbb{E}[U(a \mid b_t)]
\end{equation}
Because each candidate encapsulates both decisions jointly, the controller evaluates timing and retrieval together within a single mathematical expression.

\textbf{B9 (Decomposed):} In contrast, the decomposed baseline factorizes the choice into two sub-optimizers: one selects the binary retrieve/skip action, while the other selects the temporal trigger $\tau$. These operate as pure functions that share no state and cannot observe each other's outputs; the final executed action simply concatenates their independent decisions. It is important to emphasize that B9 is a genuine, fully realized optimizer, not a strawman. It employs the exact same objective formulation, price parameters ($\lambda_l, \lambda_c$), VoI models, and empirical constants as the coupled approach.
\textbf{The Mechanism of Divergence:} This structural difference introduces a specific mechanical divergence. Because B9's retrieval sub-optimizer cannot condition on the timing sub-optimizer's output, it must price the retrieval action at its full, unmitigated latency ($672\text{ ms}$). It structurally cannot credit the $\approx 188\text{ ms}$ of exposed latency that an early timing policy would successfully hide by overlapping with the user's speech. Consequently, B9 systematically overprices retrieval, potentially forcing a $\text{skip}$ for queries where the true net utility—if paired with optimal early timing—would be positive. Meanwhile, B9's timing optimizer selects $\tau$ based on an assumed prior of retrieval, oblivious to what the retrieval sub-optimizer actually decided.

The result is that B9's two sub-decisions are individually sensible but jointly incoherent. The joint candidate evaluation in our coupled controller is expressly designed to resolve this incoherence, allowing the retrieval decision to dynamically "see" and exploit the latency-hiding benefits of early execution. Furthermore, because the quality channel is empirically inactive across timing choices (as established in Section~\ref{sec:priced_objective}), this proposed mechanism operates entirely through the latency channel. 

Whether resolving this theoretical incoherence translates into a meaningfully superior quality-latency-compute trade-off in practice is the central tested hypothesis of our evaluation.
