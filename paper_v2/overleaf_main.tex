\begin{abstract}
A streaming voice dialogue system must decide whether to retrieve external
knowledge and when to dispatch the query while the user is still speaking. We
ask whether optimizing these two decisions jointly outperforms a decomposed
controller that faces the same latency and compute prices. Under
timing-invariant quality, constant latency overlap and a dispatch-invariant
belief (A1--A3), we prove that the joint controller at any latency price makes
the same decisions as the decomposed controller at a mapped price, so their
frontiers coincide. In a text-based experiment that satisfies A1--A3 by construction, the price map reproduces
$10{,}000/10{,}000$ off-grid decisions, and a decomposed rule that prices the
expected exposed latency reproduces the joint rule on $11{,}300/11{,}300$
turns. Turn-dependent overlap yields only second-order gains; the first-order
channel separates the two controllers only when a dispatched retrieval is
committed, and under speculative prefetch a belief-gated decomposed
prefetcher stays within a small bounded term of the joint optimum. We then ran
a pre-registered boundary test on open-domain and customer-support speech
with live automatic speech recognition (ASR) partials. On 3,734 open-domain
test questions the joint controller was worse than the strongest decomposed
rival under commitment (utility difference $-0.037$, 95\% CI
$[-0.048,-0.025]$): the boundary hypotheses were not supported and the
pre-registered reverse hypothesis held. Customer-support and pooled results
point the same way but are exploratory. The gap disappears on clean text,
implicating recognition errors in early partial transcripts and a weak belief
signal.
\end{abstract}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Spoken Dialogue Systems, Decision-Theoretic Control, Speculative Prefetch, Streaming Retrieval.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A voice assistant that grounds answers in external documents must decide whether to retrieve on a turn and when to dispatch retrieval relative to ongoing speech. Streaming systems already retrieve mid-speech, either speculatively at fixed intervals or when triggered by the model, and reduce latency without losing accuracy \cite{arora2026streamrag}. Speculative execution likewise hides tool latency in voice agents \cite{jung2026hiding}. Retrieval is not always beneficial, however. Adaptive methods decide whether to search \cite{jiang2023flare,su2024dragin,jeong2024adaptiverag}, and retrieved documents can degrade answers the language model would otherwise get right \cite{mallen2023trust}. Work on timing typically dispatches once user intent stabilizes and assumes retrieval is required \cite{galbraith2026streaming}. As a result, the two decisions are studied as separate problems.

This raises an architectural question: must the two decisions be made jointly by a coupled controller, or does a decomposed controller facing the same latency and compute prices perform equally well? In a controlled text-based experiment, a coupled controller achieved strict dominance over a price-aware decomposed baseline in 0 of 5 seeds (Section~\ref{sec:res-equiv}). We show this is not a matter of statistical power. When the timing and belief assumptions A1--A3 hold, joint control and price-mapped decomposed control have identical decision frontiers. We formalize this equivalence and identify the conditions under which the two architectures diverge.

Our approach builds on uncertainty-triggered retrieval \cite{jiang2023flare,su2024dragin}, speculative dispatch on partial transcripts \cite{arora2026streamrag,jung2026hiding}, and internal knowledge probing \cite{kadavath2022language,moskvoretskii2025adaptive}. We make five contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$C_1$ (Theory):} To our knowledge, the first formal equivalence analysis of joint and decomposed control under A1--A3. We derive an exact price map (Prop.~\ref{prop:eq}) and a fixed-price gap (Prop.~\ref{prop:gap}), and show that joint control yields first-order gains mainly under mandatory execution commitment.
    \item \textbf{$C_2$ (Boundary evaluation):} A pre-registered intersection-union test (P1--P3) of controller divergence under the commit and speculative architectures, together with a pre-registered reverse hypothesis (H-REV). The joint controller loses under commitment, so the boundary claim is not supported (Section~\ref{sec:res-stream}).
    \item \textbf{$C_3$ (Calibrated objective):} A price-aware objective that accounts for measured retrieval harm on turns that do not need retrieval ($g^- = 0.592$ vs.\ $g^+ = 0.720$).
    \item \textbf{$C_4$ (Belief dynamics):} An analysis of belief signals on partial transcripts. On customer-support turns the entropy signal is near chance (AUC $0.51$), while a self-knowledge probe reaches $0.70$ and sharpens as more of the question is heard; on open-domain questions entropy remains the stronger signal ($0.71$ vs.\ $0.67$). The support-set results are exploratory.
    \item \textbf{$C_5$ (Spoken benchmark):} A matched-pair spoken support benchmark spanning multiple organizations, in which retrieval need cannot be recovered from surface form (leak-test AUC $0.576$, 95\% CI $[0.541, 0.617]$). We release it with our open-domain set, the code and the frozen pre-registration.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/SOYL-AI/voice-rag-controller} (tag \texttt{v2-final}).}
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Streaming and speculative retrieval.} Stream RAG issues retrieval and
tool calls while the user is still speaking, comparing speculative calls at
fixed intervals with model-triggered calls \cite{arora2026streamrag}; these
correspond to our speculative and commit architectures. Speculative execution
also hides tool latency in on-device voice agents \cite{jung2026hiding}.
Galbraith characterizes when tool intent stabilizes on a streaming transcript,
but studies timing alone and assumes every query needs retrieval
\cite{galbraith2026streaming}. MoshiRAG learns whether to retrieve in a
full-duplex speech model, but retrieves only at response onset
\cite{chien2026moshirag}. To our knowledge, none asks whether deciding whether
and when to retrieve jointly beats deciding them separately under shared
prices.

\textbf{Adaptive retrieval and self-knowledge.} FLARE and DRAGIN trigger
retrieval from generation uncertainty \cite{jiang2023flare,su2024dragin},
Adaptive-RAG routes queries by predicted complexity \cite{jeong2024adaptiverag},
and simple uncertainty triggers remain competitive
\cite{moskvoretskii2025adaptive}. Models can predict whether they know an
answer \cite{kadavath2022language}; our self-knowledge probe belongs to this
family. Retrieval can also hurt on questions the model already answers
correctly \cite{mallen2023trust}, which motivates the harm term $g^{-}$.

\textbf{Benchmarks and decision theory.} $\tau$-Voice benchmarks full-duplex
voice agents on real-world domains \cite{ray2026tauvoice}; we draw open-domain
questions from PopQA \cite{mallen2023trust} and HotpotQA
\cite{yang2018hotpotqa}. Voice Activity Projection predicts turn shifts over a
short horizon \cite{ekstedt2022vap}; we instead predict the remaining speech
of a whole question from the partial transcript. Value of information
\cite{howard1966information} and decision-theoretic turn-taking
\cite{bohus2011decisions} underlie our objective; what is new is the exact
characterization of when joint and decomposed control coincide and where they
diverge.

\section{Problem Formulation and Controllers}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{subsec:problem_formulation}

While the user speaks, a streaming recogniser emits partial transcripts
$x_1,\dots,x_K$; $x_K$ is the transcript at the voice-activity (VAD) end of
turn. For each turn the assistant decides \emph{whether} to retrieve
documents or answer closed-book, and \emph{when} to send the retrieval
request. A request sent during speech overlaps the remaining speech and hides
part of its latency, but it is built from an incomplete question. After each
partial the system updates a belief $b_k$ that the turn needs retrieval and a
prediction $\hat R_k$ of the remaining speech. The controller then
dispatches, waits, or (at the end of the turn) skips. Passages go to the
generator (Mistral-7B), whose answer is spoken by TTS. User-perceived delay
is the time to first audio,
$\mathrm{TTFA}=t_{\text{first audio}}-t_{\text{user end}}$.
All controllers maximise the same priced objective
(Section~\ref{subsec:priced_objective}); they differ only in whether the two
decisions are made jointly or by separate modules
(Section~\ref{subsec:decision_rule}). Fig.~\ref{fig:system_flow} shows the
pipeline and the three decision structures we compare.

\subsection{Belief State}
\label{subsec:belief_state}

\textit{Text experiment.} The belief is computed once per turn from the full
question $q$: the signal is the mean per-token entropy (nats) of a closed-book
draft, averaged over five drafts,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}H\!\left(p_\theta(\cdot\mid y_{<i},q)\right),\\
b&=\max\!\big(0.05,\,\sigma(ws+c)\big),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:belief}
\end{equation}
with $w=11.288$, $c=-3.633$ fitted in-sample on the 36-item belief set
(180 samples, five seeds). Maximum entropy ranked items better
(Section~\ref{sec:res-equiv}), but the mean is smoother and suits a
logistic fit. Because it is computed once from the full question, this
belief does not depend on dispatch time.

\textit{Streaming experiment.} The belief is recomputed on every partial $x_k$. Mistral-7B is
asked whether the question can be answered from general knowledge alone,
without the organisation's own information; the signal is the probability it
gives to ``No'' (one generated token). It is calibrated per dataset on the
development split only, as a logistic function of the signal's logit, the
fraction of the question heard, and their interaction. An item needs
retrieval when its closed-book answer is wrong in at least three of five
drafts.

\subsection{Actions and Dispatch Timing}
\label{subsec:action_space}

In the text experiment the action is skip or retrieve with one of three
timing policies, applied to text read at 150 words per minute ($T$ = question duration;
$t_{\text{ready}}$ = first time the prefix has four words including a content
word; $t_1$ = first partial). \textsc{sentence\_end} dispatches at $T$;
\textsc{immediate} at the first partial after $t_{\text{ready}}$;
\textsc{fixed\_early\_proxy} at $\max(0.35T, t_{\text{ready}})$, with $0.35T$
capped at $0.8\,t_1$. Neither early rule forecasts the end of the turn. In the streaming
experiment the controller may dispatch at any partial, so timing becomes an
optimal-stopping problem. We test two architectures on the same items and
prices: under \textsc{commit} a dispatched retrieval is always used; under
\textsc{speculative} it may be discarded at the end of the turn, at the cost
of its compute.

\subsection{Latency- and Compute-Priced Objective}
\label{subsec:priced_objective}

Retrieving with belief $b$, dispatched at $t$, has utility
\begin{equation}
U_t(b)=b\,g^{+}(1-\rho_t)-(1-b)\,g^{-}-\lambda_l e_t-\lambda_c(1+w_t),
\label{eq:obj}
\end{equation}
and skipping has $U(\text{skip})=0$. Here $g^{+}$ is the accuracy gained by retrieving on turns that need it,
$g^{-}$ the accuracy lost on turns that do not, $\rho_t$ the share of $g^{+}$
lost to a partial query, $\rho(t)=1-\text{hit@3}(t)/\text{hit@3}(\text{end})$,
$e_t$ the exposed latency (s), $w_t$ the expected wasted retrievals, and
$\lambda_l$, $\lambda_c$ the prices of latency and compute in accuracy units.

A common simplification sets $g^{+}=1$, $g^{-}=\rho_t=0$, i.e.\ it uses $b$
itself as the gain and assumes retrieval never hurts. In the text experiment
retrieval
added $g^{+}=0.720$ [0.504, 0.888] on needed turns but cost $g^{-}=0.592$
[0.408, 0.768] on the others, so at zero prices one should retrieve only when
$b>g^{-}/(g^{+}+g^{-})=0.451$, not when $b>0.05$. In the streaming experiment, $g^{\pm}$ are
estimated on the development split: $\hat g^{+}=0.441$, $\hat g^{-}=-0.112$
on \dset{d1\_open} (retrieval did not hurt) and $0.445$, $0.034$ on
\dset{d2\_support}.

With fixed timing policies the exposure is $e_t=L-o_t$, with a fixed overlap
$o_t$ per policy. In the streaming experiment it depends on the turn,
\begin{equation}
e_k=\mathbb{E}_L\!\left[\max(0,\ L-W_k)\right]+\pi,
\label{eq:exposure}
\end{equation}
where $L$ is the retrieval latency, $W_k=\hat R_k+d$ the predicted remaining
speech plus end-of-turn detection delay ($W_K=0$), and $\pi=371$~ms the cost
no timing can hide (mainly the longer prompt). $L$ is taken as the end-to-end
TTFA increment measured on the development split for three profiles (query
rewrite plus FAISS, plus re-ranking, or a remote API; medians 1{,}044,
1{,}043 and 1{,}241~ms). The waste term $w_t$ is not measured in either
experiment: the text experiment uses fixed placeholders (0.02 and 0.08 for
the two early policies, 0 at sentence end), and the streaming experiment uses
$w(f)=0.1(1-f)$ for a dispatch made with a
fraction $f$ of the question heard ($w=0$ at the end of the turn). It enters
only the decision; the scored utility charges $\lambda_c$ per dispatched
retrieval. Section~\ref{sec:res-secondary} reports a sensitivity check over
$w\in\{0,0.04,0.08,0.16\}$. We fix $\lambda_c=0.05$. The text experiment sweeps
$\lambda_l$; the streaming test uses one pre-registered price per dataset,
$\lambda_l=0.173$ (\dset{d1\_open}) and $0.216$ (\dset{d2\_support}) per
second.

\subsection{Controllers}
\label{subsec:decision_rule}

Table~\ref{tab:controllers} lists the controllers. All priced controllers
share Eq.~(\ref{eq:obj}), the belief calibration, the remaining-speech
predictor, the latency model and $g^{\pm}$. In the streaming experiment all
baseline thresholds and timing rules are tuned on the development split with
an equal budget (the text experiment used fixed defaults).

\textbf{Ours} (text experiment) scores skip and every (retrieve, $t$) pair
and takes the best. \textbf{B9} splits the choice into a timing module and a retrieval
module that share no state, so it prices retrieval at the full latency $L$
and can skip turns that early dispatch would make worthwhile. \textbf{B9+}
keeps the split but prices the expected exposure of its own timing choice.
When the belief does not depend on dispatch time and the overlap is the same
for every turn (assumptions A1--A3, Section~\ref{sec:theory}), B9+ and Ours
are equivalent; in the text experiment they agreed on all 11{,}300
decisions. A gain from
joint control can therefore only come from turns where these assumptions
fail.

\textbf{Ours-S} (streaming; Algorithm~\ref{alg:oursv2}) compares, at each partial, the
value of dispatching now with the expected value of waiting. The next
partial carries new information with probability $P(R>s)$, where $s$ is the
median gap between partials; belief transitions are fitted on the
development split, and the lookahead depth was fixed before the test. Under
\textsc{speculative} the same rule values having the passages ready.
\textbf{B10} (SPEC) always prefetches at its trigger and uses the passages
only if $U(b_K)>0$; \textbf{B11} (SPEC-$\theta$) prefetches only when a
belief-only estimate says the prefetch is worth it.

\section{Theory}\label{sec:theory}
% P-IV.1  The three assumptions in words, and why the text experiment meets each by design
%         (frozen query, so rho = 0; one overlap per policy: 0, 0.050, 0.188 s;
%         one belief per turn from the full question).
% PROOFS: all proofs are in the public repository (footnote in Sec. I).

Write $\kappa_t=\lambda_l e_t+\lambda_c(1+w_t)$, so that
$U_t(b)=b\,g^{+}(1-\rho_t)-(1-b)\,g^{-}-\kappa_t$. The equivalence regime assumes
\textbf{(A1)} $\rho_t\equiv\bar\rho$;
\textbf{(A2)} $e_t$ is constant across turns;
\textbf{(A3)} one belief per turn, independent of dispatch time.
Under (A1) every slope is $s=g^{+}(1-\bar\rho)+g^{-}$. Let
$t^{*}=\arg\min_t\kappa_t$, $c_O=\kappa_{t^{*}}$ and
$\lambda_l'=[c_O-\lambda_c(1+w_{\mathrm{se}})]/L$.

\begin{proposition}[Equivalence]\label{prop:eq}
Assume (A1)--(A3) and $w_{\mathrm{se}}=\min_tw_t$. For every $b\in[0,1]$, Ours
at $(\lambda_l,\lambda_c)$ retrieves iff B9 at $(\lambda_l',\lambda_c)$
retrieves. The map $\lambda_l\mapsto\lambda_l'$ is a continuous, strictly
increasing bijection of $[0,\infty)$. When the maximal-overlap timing is optimal,
\[
\lambda_l'=\lambda_l\frac{L-o^{*}}{L}+\lambda_c\frac{w^{*}-w_{\mathrm{se}}}{L}.
\]
Conditional on retrieving, the timings coincide unless $\lambda_l$ and
$\lambda_l'$ lie in different linear pieces of $\lambda\mapsto\min_t\kappa_t$.
\end{proposition}
% P-IV.2  Prop. 1 in words; B9+ (pricing expected exposure) IS the joint
%         controller under A1--A3 (concedes R1's point and makes it a result).

\begin{proposition}[Gap at a fixed price]\label{prop:gap}
Assume (A1)--(A3) and model correctness: $\mathbb E[N\mid b]=b$, and realized
quality, latency and compute in expectation equal to those in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:obj}). Let $c_B=\lambda_lL+\lambda_c(1+w_{\mathrm{se}})$ and
$\mathcal B=(\theta_O,\theta_B]$ with $\theta_O=(c_O+g^{-})/s$ and
$\theta_B=(c_B+g^{-})/s$. Then
\begin{align*}
0\le\mathbb E[U_{\mathrm{Ours}}-U_{\mathrm{B9}}]
&\le(c_B-c_O)\Pr(b\in\mathcal B)\\
&\le\lambda_lo^{*}\Pr(b\in\mathcal B).
\end{align*}
For B9+ at the same price the difference is $0$ pointwise.
\end{proposition}
% P-IV.3  The divergence band B and why the bound is about the controller's
%         model, not outcomes (without model correctness the gap can be < 0).

\textbf{Channels that break the equivalence.} Dropping (A2) (turn-dependent
overlap, C1) yields a gain of order $\lambda_l^{2}\operatorname{Var}e^{*}$,
i.e.\ second order. Dropping (A1) alone (partial-query risk, C3) makes the
optimal timing depend on $b$ but keeps the joint optimum a function of $b$, so
it does not separate the two classes. Dropping (A3) (information vs.\
overlap, C2) is first order: for a committed retrieval with value $D_k$ at
partial $k$, the value of waiting
$W_k=\mathbb E[D_{k+1}^{+}\mid\mathcal F_k]-D_k^{+}$ can be positive on some
turns and negative on others that a belief-free timing rule cannot tell apart,
and then every decomposed policy is strictly suboptimal. Under speculative
prefetch, the joint gain over always-prefetch (B10) is at most the compute
spent on prefetches the joint controller would skip plus a partial-query
term, and a belief-gated prefetcher (B11) comes within a C1-sized term of the
joint optimum. These predictions define the pre-registered tests: P1, joint
beats \Bplus{} under commit ($\DC>0$); P2, the advantage shrinks under
speculation ($\DC-\DS>0$); P3, under speculation the joint controller is
within $m_3=0.016$ of the best decomposed rival (equivalence test); and
H-REV, $\DC<0$, added before the freeze because the development data pointed
that way. The boundary claim requires P1, P2 and P3 together.
